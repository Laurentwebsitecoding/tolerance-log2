Why weight

The number everyone already acts on, and almost nobody records

Tolerance is the mechanism. Weight is the outcome. The log is the first thing that holds both at once, which is why weight belongs in it.

01

It starts before treatment does

One in five head and neck patients already has critical weight loss at diagnosis, and in tumours of the lower throat it is more than four in ten. Over half of patients without metastases have lost weight by their first oncology visit.

Jager-Wittenaar et al. 2007 (UMCG, 407 patients); Muscaritoli et al. 2017 (PreMiO)
02

Treatment makes it worse

Up to 88% of head and neck patients can become malnourished during chemoradiation. Across cancer patients, 20 to 30% may die from the consequences of malnutrition rather than from the cancer.

Löser et al. 2024 (SUFEETUBE); Caccialanza et al. 2020
03

Weight loss changes the treatment

Patients who lose weight are 1.3 to 1.7 times more likely to have their systemic treatment modified. That is the moment nutrition stops being supportive care and starts affecting the chance of cure.

Shaw et al. 2020
04

Feeding early protects both

Head and neck patients who started tube feeding before treatment lost 2.4% of their weight against 15.2% without a tube, and completed 96% of their chemotherapy against 81.7%. Fewer emergency visits too.

Lewis et al. 2014
05

But only if the feed is tolerated

Around 40% of tube-fed cancer patients still do not reach their protein and energy targets, and 30.5% of tube-fed intensive care patients develop feed intolerance. A feed that is prescribed is not yet a feed that is delivered.

De Souza et al. 2018; critical care cohort on enteral feed intolerance
06

And almost nobody measures it

77% of German head and neck physicians do not screen nutrition routinely before chemoradiation. The chain above is well evidenced and barely instrumented. A weekly weight and a daily symptom score close that gap at almost no cost.

Löser et al. 2024

One thing we deliberately left out

Dietitians watch for a 5% loss, because that is where malnutrition criteria start. We considered drawing that line on the weight chart and decided not to. A threshold is a comparison against a norm, and that is one of the four things that would turn this record into regulated clinical software. The chart shows the weight and the percentage change; the dietitian knows where the line sits.

How it works

One minute a day, at the kitchen table

A working prototype. Log a day, then open History and Summary at the bottom to see what the family gets back.

01

The family logs, not the dietitian

She is the bottleneck the tool exists to relieve, so it never adds to her day. A parent is already watching for these symptoms and already keeping notes.

02

Every brand on the Dutch market is in the list

Nutrison, Nutrini and NutriniMax from Nutricia; Isosource and Compleat from Nestlé Health Science; Fresubin and NutriChef from Fresenius Kabi; the HiPP range; and Nuvacal. A log that only records one manufacturer's feed is a marketing device. One that records them all is a clinical instrument, and only the second convinces a procurement team.

03

No name ever enters the log

The family opens it from a link tied to a code the hospital issued. The list that connects that code to a patient stays inside the hospital, and a supplier never holds it.

04

It records, it does not advise

No alerts, no risk scores, no suggested feed. That restraint is what keeps it a record rather than clinical software, and it is a deliberate design choice rather than a missing feature.

05

Nobody switches overnight

Dietitians introduce a new feed in steps — a quarter of the day's volume, then half, then all of it, over one to three weeks. So the log records what actually went in each day rather than which product the patient is “on”. The changeover shows as a band on every chart, and the before-and-after comparison uses only the days at nought and at a hundred percent. So the log asks two things: which feed is being left and which is being started, and how much of today’s millilitres was which. The two parts always add up to the volume actually given.

06

Paper works too

The asset is the standard way of recording, not the software. A printed card and a monthly summary prove the same thing at a fraction of the cost, which is what a first pilot can actually run.

Staying with it

Apps in healthcare die from abandonment, not rejection

Seven weeks of chemoradiation is a long time to ask anything of someone whose mouth hurts. These are the levers we would rely on, strongest first. The cheapest ones are also the best ones.

01

Twenty seconds a day

Dull, and the single biggest lever there is. Every extra field costs adherence. In a population with mucositis and fatigue this outranks every clever mechanic, which is why the daily entry is five taps and two sliders.

No approval needed
02

Your dietitian sees this on Thursday

Accountability inside a real clinical relationship beats any points system. These patients are in clinic every week during treatment, so the summary lands the evening before the appointment and gets opened in front of them.

No approval needed
03

Your seven weeks become the evidence for the next patient

Cancer patients consistently say they want their experience to count for something. Here it is literally true: this group is small, and a few dozen complete records are the difference between an opinion and a finding.

No approval needed
04

Week 4 of 7, not a streak

Progress that maps onto the treatment they want to finish. A brittle streak does the opposite: break it on a bad day and people quit, and here a bad day is a symptom of a serious illness. Nothing in the app ever scolds.

No approval needed
05

The data itself as the reward

After two weeks there is enough to show someone their own pattern. Self-knowledge is the most durable motivator there is, and it costs nothing to give away.

No approval needed
06

A reminder at a time they choose

Unglamorous and very effective. Chosen by the patient, not set by us, and silenceable without guilt.

No approval needed
07

A prize draw

Worth doing, but last on this list rather than first. Extrinsic rewards work in the short term and can crowd out the intrinsic ones above. It is also the only item here that needs sign-off before it can run.

Why the Antoni van Leeuwenhoek first

The AVL is the Netherlands' dedicated cancer hospital and one of its eight head and neck oncology centres. Around 905 newly diagnosed patients a year there face serious tube-feeding needs: over 600 with head and neck cancer, 186 with oesophageal and 119 with stomach cancer. It already states that cancer care is about more than survival numbers, and it backs that with a Centre for Quality of Life bringing together 14 clinical disciplines, oncology dietitians and speech therapists among them.

A focused hospital that already measures quality of life is more likely to buy on outcome than a large network buying on volume. The entry point is the dietitian, not the patient: she hands out access when tube feeding starts.

Ready for value-based procurementQuality of life is a strategic goal with its own department, so outcomes rather than unit price can drive the choice.
The right team in one placeDietitians, speech therapists and psychologists already work side by side — natural users of one shared record.
A fit for the productAn energy- and protein-dense feed plus tolerance data gives the centre evidence it wants to collect anyway.
Then the networkAfter one documented pilot, the head and neck dietetic network reaches the other 13 centres. Belgium is the logical next country; Germany after that.

Questions

Four things that have to be true first

A supplier collecting evidence on its own product is the obvious objection, and the honest answer is to design it out before anyone raises it.

Who can see the data?

No name, address or date of birth ever enters the log. The hospital issues each patient a code, the family logs against that code, and the list that connects the two stays in the hospital's own system. A supplier never receives it and so cannot work back to a person.

01The hospital issues a code, for example NL-0442, and keeps the only list that links it to a patient.
02The family logs symptoms, volume and feed against that code. No identifying field exists in the form.
03The dietitian sees her own patients because she has access to that list; nobody outside the hospital does.
04What leaves the hospital is coded or counted — group totals, not rows that belong to a person.

Call it pseudonymised, not anonymous. Under the GDPR, coded data is still personal data as long as a key exists somewhere, and with a few dozen children in one centre a date and a condition can narrow things down fast. Claiming full anonymity would not survive a conversation with a hospital privacy officer, and the design does not need it to: the point is that the key never leaves the hospital.

Independence matters for the same reason. Four insurers stopped reimbursing a medical nutrition product in 2013 after questions about sponsor-funded evidence, and a log run by a manufacturer invites exactly that. So the analysis plan is fixed before the pilot starts and publication is agreed in writing regardless of the result — including a result where a Nuvacal product performs no better.

Why isn't this a medical device?

Software is only a medical device if it has a medical purpose of its own. European guidance on qualifying software (MDCG 2019-11) draws the line at whether the software acts on the data: a product that stores, displays, communicates or searches what someone entered is not a device, while one that interprets it for a clinical decision usually is.

This version sits firmly on the first side, and the things it deliberately does not do are what keep it there:

  • No risk score. It adds up the five numbers the family typed. Arithmetic on the user's own entries is not interpretation.
  • No thresholds or alerts. Nothing turns red, nothing notifies anyone, nothing says a value is too high.
  • No comparison against a norm. It never tells you whether a score is normal for a seven-year-old.
  • No recommendation or prediction. It never suggests a feed, never ranks one above another, never forecasts anything.

What remains is a diary with a chart on top. The dietitian supplies every clinical judgement; the tool supplies the record she is judging. Add any one of the four above and this stops being true — software meant to inform a treatment decision falls under Rule 11 and is normally Class IIa or higher, which is a different project with a different budget.

One caution. Intended purpose is set by what the maker claims, not only by what the code does. A marketing line promising to find the right feed could create a medical purpose on its own, so the wording on a real product page needs the same review as the software. The headline on this concept page is exactly the kind of claim a specialist should look at.

Does this need ethics approval?

The hospital decides, and that conversation has to happen before anyone is enrolled.

A project that collects routine care data to improve care is often non-WMO — outside the Dutch Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act — because patients are not subjected to an intervention or made to follow imposed behaviour. The feed is chosen by the dietitian exactly as it would be anyway; the log records what happened.

Two things would change that answer. Requiring patients to log as a study procedure starts to look like imposed behaviour. And adding an incentive, particularly a randomised one, makes the project a study of behaviour rather than a record of care. If the prize draw goes ahead in the form described above, assume the question has to be asked again.

So the sequence is: ask the AVL's research office or METC for a non-WMO declaration first, agree the data-processing arrangement with the hospital as controller, and only then design the incentive. Doing it the other way round costs months.

Why would anyone still be using it in week three?

Apps in healthcare die from abandonment, not rejection. The design answer is that it has to give something back rather than only take.

  • The family gets a monthly summary they can bring to the appointment, so the logging has a visible point.
  • The dietitian's page replaces notes she already writes by hand, so her day gets shorter rather than longer.
  • Days-logged-per-patient is a reported outcome of the pilot, not an afterthought. If people stop, that is the finding.
What would it show if the feed made no difference?

Exactly that, and visibly. In the data section above, open Noor: she moved to a Nuvacal product and her symptoms did not improve. Fleur changed within the same product family and nothing shifted either.

Two of the four example patients show no benefit, and that is deliberate. A log that only ever produced good news would be worth nothing to a dietitian, and a procurement team would see through it immediately. Leaving the failures in is what makes the rest of it readable.

Start with one centre, one patient group, one question.

Does this patient tolerate this feed better than the last one? Answer it a few hundred times and you have the evidence the market is missing.
